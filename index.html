01 / The investment question

Are EV incentives worth the cost?

Government support can speed up a cleaner transition. But when does a public dollar create value, and when does it simply subsidize a purchase that would happen anyway?

THE SHORT ANSWERYes, when support is targeted, temporary and additional.

03 / The additionality test

Count what the incentive changes.

The key finance question is not “How many EVs were sold?” It is “How many cleaner vehicle choices happened because of the policy?”

Value created

Benefits beyond the buyer

Lower emissions and local air pollution; less exposure to oil-price shocks; potential learning and charging-network spillovers.

Value leaked

Cost without a behavior change

Payments to buyers who would have switched anyway, plus fiscal cost, administration and benefits concentrated among households able to buy new cars.

THE TESTSocial benefits from additional EV adoption > full public cost

05 / The investment case

Make incentives earn their place.

Keep support where the market still needs a nudge. Make it targeted, predictable and designed to phase down as adoption and charging mature.

01Prioritize buyers and segments where price is still a binding barrier.
02Compare purchase credits with charging infrastructure and grid investment.
03Publish cost per additional EV and emissions avoided; sunset what underperforms.

Verdict: economically justified as a bridge, not a blank cheque.

Design implication: target market failures, measure additionality, revisit as the market changes.

06 / Sources & discussion

Good policy follows the evidence.

The conclusion is conditional: incentives make economic sense when their incremental social benefits beat their full cost, and better tools are not available.
